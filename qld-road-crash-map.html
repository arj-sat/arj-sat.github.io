Project — GIS & Data

Serious road crashes in Queensland

2020 – 2024 · ArcGIS Online · Python

An interactive map of 66,623 crashes in which someone was injured or killed on Queensland roads, built to show where the most serious crashes happen and in what conditions.

Zoom in and click a crash for its details. The legend shows how crashes are coloured by severity.

01 — About

Where do the worst crashes happen?

GIS Road Safety Open Data Web Mapping

Road safety teams have to decide where to put limited money and attention. This map is a starting point for that question: it shows every injury crash reported on Queensland roads over five years, so patterns by place, speed zone, lighting and time of day are easy to spot.

Each point is one crash, coloured by its most serious outcome: fatal, hospitalisation, medical treatment or minor injury. Clicking a crash opens a pop-up with the crash type, suburb, speed limit, lighting, road surface, time and number of casualties.

I built it as a portfolio project to practise taking a large public dataset from raw download to a published, shareable web map.

02 — Findings

What the data shows.

These patterns come from serious crashes, meaning fatal crashes and crashes with hospitalisation. They describe how outcomes differ, not how likely a crash is.

Faster roads, deadlier crashes
About 8.6% of serious crashes in 110 km/h zones were fatal, against about 2% in zones of 60 km/h or less.
Remote areas fare worst
Around 8.8% of serious crashes in remote areas were fatal, against 2.4% in major cities.
Vulnerable road users
Pedestrians, motorcyclists or cyclists were involved in 39.8% of fatal crashes, against 29.4% of all serious crashes.
Darkness adds risk
36.7% of fatal crashes happened in the dark, against 28.3% of serious crashes.
Southern Queensland
It recorded 326 fatal crashes, against 183 in the Metropolitan region, even though Metropolitan has the most serious crashes overall.
Read with care
The data has no traffic volumes, so these are counts and shares, not crash rates per kilometre driven.
03 — Method

How I built it.

001
Sourced the data
Downloaded the crash locations data published by Transport and Main Roads on the Queensland Government open data portal.
002
Cleaned it in Python
With pandas: kept 2020 to 2024, removed columns the map does not need, checked for duplicates, and confirmed every crash has coordinates inside Queensland.
003
Made it readable
Converted coded severity values into plain labels so the legend and pop-ups make sense to a general audience.
004
Published and designed the map
Published to ArcGIS Online as a hosted feature layer, then built the web map with severity symbology, pop-ups, bookmarks and a legend, and embedded it here.
